# Home Insulation: R-Value, Air Sealing and Bridges

Insulation is rated by R-value, its resistance to conductive heat flow: the higher the number, the slower heat passes through the material. What matters in a wall or roof is the whole assembly's R-value, not the batt's label — a perfectly rated blanket installed with gaps, compression and wind washing performs far below its number. Climate zones set target values; cold climates commonly call for the equivalent of R-49 in an attic, roughly 35 centimetres of mineral wool or fibreglass.

In most older houses, though, the bigger thief is moving air. Warm air leaking out through gaps around pipes, downlights, attic hatches and wall plates can carry more heat than conduction through the insulated surfaces — and it carries moisture with it, which condenses inside cold cavities and rots timber. Sealing those leaks with foam, tape and gaskets is unglamorous, cheap, and usually the highest-return work in the building. The rule of thumb: seal first, then insulate, because insulation laid over leaks filters the air without stopping it and gets dirty doing so.

Thermal bridges are the third failure. Timber studs conduct heat several times faster than the insulation between them, so a stud wall's real performance is a weighted average dragged down by its frame; steel framing bridges worse. Continuous insulation — a rigid layer over the whole exterior, breaking the bridge at every stud — is the modern answer, and even a thin continuous layer can outperform thicker cavity fill alone. Windows, finally, are holes in the thermal envelope by design: their ratings matter, but so do the seals at their edges.
