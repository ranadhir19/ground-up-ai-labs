# Kombucha: The Culture in the Jar

Kombucha is sweet tea fermented by a SCOBY — a symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast that forms a pale, rubbery mat floating on the brew. The yeast converts sugar to alcohol and carbon dioxide; the bacteria convert much of the alcohol to acetic acid, which gives kombucha its vinegar tang and protects the brew from mould. A batch at room temperature takes seven to fourteen days: shorter for a sweet, mild drink, longer for a dry, sharp one.

A healthy culture smells cleanly vinegary. The stringy brown strands and cloudy patches that alarm beginners are usually just yeast colonies and new SCOBY growth. The genuine danger signs are mould — dry, fuzzy spots in green, black or pink sitting on the surface — and a smell of rot rather than vinegar. A mouldy batch is thrown out entirely, culture and all; there is no rescuing it.

Flavouring happens in a second ferment, after the SCOBY is removed to a jar of starter tea. Fruit, ginger or herbs go into sealed bottles with the finished kombucha for two to four days, where trapped carbon dioxide builds the fizz. The bottles are a small pressure hazard: burp them daily, and never leave a sealed second ferment forgotten in a warm room. The reserved starter tea, kept acidic, is both the seed of the next batch and the culture's lifeboat.
