# Sail Trim: Reading Telltales and Shaping Draft

Telltales — short lengths of yarn or ribbon attached to both sides of a sail — are the cheapest wind instruments on a boat. When a sail is trimmed correctly, the telltales on both surfaces stream straight back, showing smooth, attached airflow. If the windward telltale lifts and dances, the sail is trimmed too far out or the boat is pointing too high; if the leeward one stalls and droops behind the sail, the sheet needs easing. Steering and trimming by telltales, rather than by feel alone, is what separates a boat that slips along from one that wallows.

The sail's power lives in its draft — the depth of its curve. A fuller sail generates more power for light winds and choppy water; a flatter sail depowers the boat when it is over-canvassed. Halyard and Cunningham tension pull the draft forward and flatten the sail; outhaul tension flattens the lower third. On a beat, the traveller and mainsheet work together: ease the traveller in a gust to spill wind without disturbing the sail's shape, rather than dumping the sheet and losing the trim you had.

Points of sail organise everything else. Close-hauled, sails are sheeted hard in and the boat sails at roughly 45 degrees to the true wind — it cannot sail directly into the wind, and attempts to point higher simply stall the sails. A reach frees the sheets and is the fastest, most comfortable point for most boats. Dead downwind, the main is eased fully out, the headsail may be poled out on the opposite side, and the danger is the accidental gybe: the boom swinging across uncontrolled, which is why a preventer line is rigged whenever the run is more than a few minutes.
