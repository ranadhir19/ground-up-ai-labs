# Composting: Turning Waste into Soil

A compost pile is a managed habitat for bacteria and fungi. The microbes need carbon for energy and nitrogen for protein, and the working ratio is roughly three parts carbon-rich "browns" — dry leaves, straw, shredded cardboard — to one part nitrogen-rich "greens" such as grass clippings, coffee grounds and vegetable scraps. Too much green material and the pile turns slimy and smells of ammonia, which is excess nitrogen escaping as gas. The fix is to fork in more browns and turn the pile to restore air.

Moisture matters as much as the recipe. The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge: damp throughout, never dripping. A heap that dries out stops decomposing entirely, because the microbes go dormant; a heap that is waterlogged excludes oxygen and switches to anaerobic bacteria, which work slowly and produce sour, swampy odours. A simple cover keeps rain from soaking the pile while letting it breathe.

Heat is the sign the system is working. A well-built cubic metre of material will reach 55 to 65 degrees Celsius in the centre within a week, hot enough to kill most weed seeds and pathogens. Turning the pile every week or two moves cool outer material into the hot core and recharges the oxygen. When the pile stops reheating after a turn and the contents look like dark, crumbly soil with no recognisable scraps, the compost is finished.
