# Sourdough: Starters, Bulk Fermentation and Proofing

A sourdough starter is a culture of wild yeast and lactobacillus bacteria kept alive in a jar of flour and water. Fed at room temperature, it wants a meal of fresh flour and water once a day; kept in the fridge, once a week is enough. Most feedings use equal weights of flour and water — a 1:1:1 ratio of starter to flour to water — and a healthy starter reliably doubles in volume within four to eight hours of feeding, smells pleasantly tangy rather than of nail polish, and passes the float test: a spoonful dropped in water floats when it is full of gas and ready to raise dough.

Bulk fermentation is where the loaf is really made. After mixing, the dough rests and ferments as one mass while the baker stretches and folds it every half hour or so to build strength. The clock is a poor guide — a warm kitchen at 26 degrees may finish bulk in three hours, a cool one at 20 degrees may take six — so bakers watch the dough instead: it should grow by roughly half, feel smooth and elastic, and show bubbles at the surface and through the sides of the container. Under-fermented dough bakes up dense with a wild, tunnelling crumb; over-fermented dough loses structure and spreads flat in the oven.

Shaping and proofing finish the process. The dough is shaped to build surface tension, placed seam-up in a floured basket, and proofed — often overnight in the fridge, where the cold retards the yeast while the bacteria keep producing the acids that give sourdough its flavour. It bakes in a preheated covered pot, typically 20 minutes with the lid on for steam and oven spring, then uncovered until the crust is deeply coloured and the loaf sounds hollow when thumped.
