# Soil pH: Testing, Adjusting and Plant Preferences

Soil pH measures acidity on a scale where 7 is neutral, lower is acid and higher is alkaline. The scale is logarithmic, so a soil at pH 5 is ten times as acidic as one at pH 6 — small numbers are big changes. pH matters because it governs nutrient availability: in strongly acid soil, phosphorus locks onto iron and aluminium and becomes unavailable to roots, while in alkaline soil iron and manganese become scarce and leaves yellow between the veins despite the elements being present.

Test before you amend. A laboratory test through an extension service costs little and reports not just pH but the soil's buffer capacity — its resistance to change — which decides how much amendment you need. Sandy soils shift easily; clay soils, heavily buffered, may need several times as much material for the same movement. Cheap probe meters are unreliable; if you test at home, use a dye kit or a calibrated meter, sample from several spots at root depth, and mix them into one composite sample.

To raise pH, gardeners apply lime — ground limestone, or dolomite lime where magnesium is also low — typically in autumn so it has months to react. To lower pH, elemental sulphur is the standard tool: soil bacteria oxidise it to sulphuric acid over a season, gradually acidifying the bed. Blueberries, azaleas and rhododendrons want genuinely acid soil, around 4.5 to 5.5, and chronic yellowing of their leaves in ordinary garden soil is usually the plant reporting that the pH, not the feeding, is wrong. Most vegetables are content between 6.0 and 7.0.
