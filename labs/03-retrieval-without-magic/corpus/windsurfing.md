# Windsurfing: Power in the Hands

A windsurfer steers with the sail, not a rudder: there is no rudder. The board is turned by moving the rig — tilt the mast forward and the board bears away from the wind; rake it back and the board heads up toward it — because the sail's centre of effort shifts relative to the fin's centre of resistance. This is the first and strangest lesson for sailors arriving from dinghies: your hands hold both the engine and the steering wheel, and the engine is a wing you can spill power from instantly by sheeting out.

Getting going is a choreography of balance. The uphaul lifts the rig from the water; the sailor stands over the board's centreline, mast foot between the feet, and sheets in gently until the sail fills and begins to pull. The harness — lines from the boom clipped to a hook at the waist — arrives later, transferring the sail's pull from tiring arms to the body's weight, which the sailor commits outward, leaning against the wind. Beach starts and water starts replace the uphaul as skill grows: the water start, using the wind itself to lift sailor and rig from the sea, is the gateway skill to strong-wind sailing.

Planing is the threshold everyone chases. Below it the board pushes water aside like a boat; above a certain speed it climbs onto its own bow wave and skims, drag falls away, and the board accelerates violently into a different sport. Footstraps, useless while plodding, become essential at planing speed to keep the board attached to its rider. Conditions choose the sail size — too much canvas in a gust is not faster, just louder, wetter and more exhausting.
