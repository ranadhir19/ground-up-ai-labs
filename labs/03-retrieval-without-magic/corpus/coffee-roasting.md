# Coffee Roasting: First Crack, Development and Rest

Roasting is a sequence of drying, browning and development, and the roastmaster's instruments are ears, nose and timer as much as thermometer. Green coffee goes into a hot drum and first turns yellow as it dries, smelling of hay and then of baking bread as the Maillard reactions begin. Somewhere around 196 degrees Celsius the beans fracture audibly — first crack, a sound like twigs snapping — as steam and carbon dioxide split the bean's structure. Everything before first crack is preparation; everything after is the roast itself.

Development is the stretch between first crack and the moment the beans are dropped and cooled, and its length, often expressed as a percentage of total roast time, shapes the cup. Drop too soon after first crack and the coffee tastes grassy and sour, its sugars barely formed. Develop longer and sweetness and body build — until a second, quieter crackling announces second crack, where oils migrate to the surface and the flavour tips from sweet to smoky and ashen. Most specialty roasting lives between the end of first crack and the first whisper of the second.

Freshly roasted beans are not ready to brew. They hold so much carbon dioxide that hot water cannot wet them evenly, and espresso made with day-old roast foams and extracts erratically. A rest of several days — two or three for filter coffee, a week or more for espresso — lets the gas escape. From there the clock runs the other way: whole beans are at their best within about a month, kept sealed away from light, heat and the fridge's smells.
