# Rainwater Harvesting: Catchment, First Flush and Storage

Every millimetre of rain falling on a square metre of roof yields one litre of water, so a modest 80-square-metre roof in a climate with 800 millimetres of annual rainfall catches about 64,000 litres a year. The catchment calculation is simply roof area times rainfall, discounted by a runoff coefficient — around 0.85 for tile or metal — because some water splashes, evaporates or overflows the gutters in heavy downpours.

Roof water is not clean water. The first rain after a dry spell washes dust, bird droppings and leaf litter off the surface, which is why systems fit a first-flush diverter: a vertical pipe that captures the first 20 to 40 litres of a storm and slowly drains it away, diverting only the later, cleaner flow into the tank. A mesh screen at the tank inlet stops leaves and mosquitoes, and the tank itself must be opaque, because light grows algae in stored water within weeks.

Storage sizing is a balancing act between the longest dry spell you want to bridge and the demand you place on the system. Garden irrigation is forgiving; household use is not, and in most jurisdictions rainwater for drinking needs filtration and disinfection regardless of how clean the roof looks. An overflow pipe, led well away from foundations, is not optional: a full tank in a storm moves a lot of water very quickly.
