# Backyard Pond Ecology: Oxygen, Algae and the Nitrogen Cycle

A pond is a small, loaded ecosystem, and most of its crises are oxygen crises. Oxygen enters through the surface and from submerged plants and algae in daylight; at night the same plants respire and consume it, while decomposition of waste consumes it around the clock. The daily minimum arrives just before dawn — which is why fish in trouble are found gasping at the surface in the early morning, at the hour when the water holds the least oxygen of the whole day. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen than cold, so the dangerous mornings are summer ones.

Fish waste and uneaten food release ammonia, which is toxic to fish even in small concentrations. In an established pond, colonies of bacteria on gravel, filter media and plant roots run the nitrogen cycle: one group oxidises ammonia to nitrite — also toxic — and a second group oxidises nitrite to nitrate, which plants absorb as fertiliser. These colonies take weeks to establish, which is why new ponds are stocked slowly: the water can look crystal clear while ammonia climbs invisibly, and the only way to know is to test.

Algae are the pond's unruly nutrient gauge. Green water — a bloom of single-celled algae — almost always reports excess nutrients: too many fish, too much food, too much sunlit water with too few plants competing for the load. The durable fixes attack the budget, not the bloom: feed less, shade part of the surface, add oxygenating plants, and let the filter's bacteria mature. Algaecides clear the water for a fortnight and leave the nutrients, plus a load of dead algae, behind.
